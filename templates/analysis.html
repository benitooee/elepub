Pet Adoption Scenario Analysis

This website has been created for analytical purposes, specifically to examine how different AI models respond to pet adoption scenarios while pretending to embody particular personalities. By placing the models in controlled, personality based settings, the project aims to observe the extent to which these systems demonstrate an understanding of human behaviour, decision making, and emotional reasoning.

Their responses are evaluated using several guiding questions: Do the models display coherent behavioural patterns? Do their choices align with realistic human tendencies given their background and constraints? And how effectively can they apply these “personality” traits in everyday, practical contexts such as money management and pet care? Ultimately, this analysis offers insight into the developmental maturity of contemporary AI systems and their potential applications and limitations in realistic social and domestic situations.

1. Failure Rate of LLMs Answering Questions

{% if pie_url %} {% endif %}

The pie chart summarises how often the language models failed to provide an answer across the entire questionnaire. Out of a total of 1,683 individual questions, 84 were effectively unanswered (either left blank or marked as “Not specified”), representing about 6% of all responses. The remaining 94% of questions were answered in some form.

This metric captures a very basic but important property of the models: reliability. Even if an answer is imperfect, a complete failure to respond indicates a breakdown in following instructions or dealing with certain types of prompts. Later sections focus less on “did it answer?” and more on “does the answer make sense for this persona?”, but this first chart shows that non-answers are still a noticeable minority.

2. Financial Reasoning Across Personas

{% if budget_overall_url %} {% endif %}

The overall scatter plot shows how often each monthly budget category was chosen across all personas for the main financial question (e.g. how much money they can spend on a pet’s daily needs). Each dot represents one answer; the vertical axis shows how many times that category has been selected so far, while the red line and green band show the median and interquartile range (IQR) of these counts.

We can see that the mid-range options (such as $50–$100 and $100–$200) are much more common than the extremes. This suggests that, in general, the models tend to choose “safe” middle-ground financial answers rather than very low or very high budgets—regardless of the specific personality they are role-playing.

2.1 Budget Choices by Specific Persona

{% if budget_person_url %} {% endif %}

This set of scatter plots breaks the same budget question down by individual persona:

  • Stephanie Lee – from Hong Kong, currently unemployed and actively job seeking (28 years old).
  • Raymond Lau – retired secondary school teacher from Hong Kong (60 years old).
  • Jason Lam – part-time barista from Hong Kong (24 years old).
  • Samuel Ng – Form 5 secondary school student from Hong Kong.
  • Li Wei – linguistics student (20 years old).

Each panel shows how frequently that specific persona chose each budget category. Ideally, the financial choices should reflect the persona’s situation: for example, an unemployed job-seeker or a secondary school student should probably not claim to be extremely generous or “fully prepared” financially, while a retired teacher might plausibly have more stability.

Where the plots show very similar budgets across all personas, this suggests that the models may be defaulting to generic, “nice sounding” answers instead of adapting their behaviour to the constraints of each character. This is particularly useful for criticism: if a model claims that a low-income or financially insecure persona is still “fully prepared” with a high pet budget, it exposes a lack of realistic financial reasoning.

3. Matching Pet Activity Level and Time Left Alone

{% if heatmap_url %} {% endif %}

The heatmap shows how often different combinations of pet activity level and time spent alone were chosen. The rows represent the intended activity level of the pet (from Low – calm and cuddly up to High – very energetic), while the columns indicate how many hours per day the pet would be left alone (0–4, 5–8, or 9+ hours).

Most responses concentrate in the middle of the grid: medium activity pets that are left alone for 5–8 hours per day. There are fewer cases where a high-energy pet is left alone for long periods, which is a positive sign: it suggests that the models at least partially understand that very energetic animals should not be left by themselves for too long. However, the overall pattern still shows a bias toward comfortable, moderate combinations, again raising the question of whether the models are truly reasoning about the welfare trade-offs or simply repeating “reasonable sounding” defaults.

Together with the financial charts, this heatmap helps evaluate how consistently the models balance responsibilities (time, energy, money) with the type of pet they choose. This is key to judging whether their decisions align with realistic human behaviour in everyday domestic life.
